PALAEOECOLOGY

Reading an Ediacaran
seafloor from the
spacing of its fossils

PAPER2026 · PRECAMBRIAN RESEARCH
MY ROLECO-AUTHOR
READ TIME~3 MINUTES
FORMATPLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY

Thousands of disc-shaped Beltanelliformis fossils from Moldova reveal two different ways these early communities were assembled — by currents, or by patches on the seafloor.

Disc-shaped fossils are among the most abundant and widespread organisms of the late Ediacaran (about 580–539 million years ago). What they actually were is still debated — microbial colonies, jellyfish-like medusae, or the holdfasts that anchored frond-shaped organisms to the seafloor. Two of them, Beltanelliformis and Nemiana, are often interpreted as algal or bacterial colonies and typically occur in dense clusters of a single species.

This study, led by E.G. Mitchell, asks a different kind of question. Instead of looking at individual fossils, it looks at where they are relative to each other. Spatial patterns — whether specimens are clumped, evenly spaced or random, and at what scales — preserve the imprint of the biological and ecological processes that built the community.

The surfaces

The core of the dataset comes from four newly mapped bedding surfaces in the Moldova-Podillya Basin (ca. 567–539 Ma):

  • Valcine Area A and Valcine Area B — nearshore to shallow-marine settings;
  • Cosaui — a lower-energy marine setting;
  • Egoreni — shallow-marine conditions.

Together these surfaces contain 2352 specimens over 11.46 m². For comparison we re-analysed two previously published communities from the Urals (Kolesnikov, 2022; 196 specimens over 1.80 m²) and the White Sea area (Leonov, 2007; 121 specimens over 0.46 m²).

FIG. 1 — The two community dynamics, sketched. Left: in higher-energy settings, clusters are oriented and spread by currents. Right: in quieter settings, specimens gather on favourable patches of the seafloor. Schematic, not real specimen positions.

Two ways to build a community

The communities did not all behave the same way. The Valcine and Urals surfaces show strongly oriented clusters and dispersal-driven patterns: offspring settled in clumps that were spread and aligned by water flow. The Cosaui, Egoreni and White Sea surfaces, in contrast, are best explained by habitat patchiness: specimens concentrated wherever subtle differences in the substrate made the seafloor more favourable.

These two dynamics line up with differences in depositional setting. Beltanelliformis living in higher-energy environments were organised by dispersal in currents; those in weaker flow were organised by local, subtle heterogeneities of the seafloor. We also inferred a maximum lifespan for Nemiana-Beltanelliformis comparable to, but shorter than, that of living cyanobacteria.

Why it matters

Ediacaran communities from Newfoundland and England — the so-called Avalon assemblage — show remarkably consistent ecological drivers across large distances and long time spans. The Baltica communities studied here, part of the White Sea assemblage, behave differently: their dynamics vary with the environment. That variability is itself a clue to how these early ecosystems worked, and it shows how much information is stored in a single, well-exposed bedding plane.

KEY TERMS
Beltanelliformis / NemianaDisc-shaped Ediacaran fossils, often interpreted as algal or bacterial colonies, commonly found in dense clusters.
Spatial analysisStatistical study of how specimens are arranged on a surface — clustered, random or regular — to infer the processes behind the pattern.
Bedding surfaceA preserved ancient seafloor; on it, fossils can lie where the organisms lived.
White Sea assemblageEdiacaran biota typical of shallow-marine settings, named after classic sites in northern Russia.
