GEOCHEMISTRY

Phosphorite pebbles
from an Ediacaran sea

PAPER2026 · PRECAMBRIAN RESEARCH
MY ROLELEAD AUTHOR
READ TIME~4 MINUTES
FORMATPLAIN-LANGUAGE SUMMARY

Reworked phosphorite clasts in the Dzhurzhivka Beds are rich in rare earth elements — and point to deep, phosphorus-rich water welling up onto the margin of Baltica.

Phosphorus is one of the nutrients that ultimately limits life in the ocean. When it becomes unusually abundant, it can precipitate on the seafloor as phosphorite — a rock made largely of the mineral apatite. Phosphorites from the late Ediacaran and early Cambrian are therefore small archives of ocean chemistry and nutrient cycling at a pivotal moment in the evolution of animals. More recently they have also attracted attention for a practical reason: some of them are strongly enriched in rare earth elements (REE).

In the Moldova-Podillya Basin of southwestern Baltica, these phosphorites had barely been characterised geochemically. This paper focuses on the Dzhurzhivka Beds (Nahoryany Formation, Mohyliv-Podilsky Group), where phosphorite does not occur as a continuous layer but as reworked clasts inside a conglomerate.

What the rocks look like

The conglomerates contain two components: phosphorite clasts and a phosphatic matrix, held together by a clay- and chert-rich cement. Under the microscope the clasts turn out to be phosphate-cemented sandstones with a terrigenous (land-derived) component. In other words, phosphorite first formed below the sediment–water interface, was later eroded and winnowed, and was then redeposited in localised settings of a shallow sea.

We analysed both components with bulk-rock methods (ICP-MS and XRF) and with microanalytical techniques (SEM-EDS, electron microprobe and LA-ICP-MS on individual apatite grains) to find out exactly which minerals host the phosphorus and the rare earth elements.

Rich in rare earth elements

The conglomerates are markedly REE-enriched: on average about 2053 ppm in the clasts and 1572 ppm in the matrix. The apatite grains themselves reach an average of about 5257 ppm. A strong correlation between REE and P2O5 confirms that fluorapatite is the main host.

The REE pattern has a distinctive “hat” shape, with the middle rare earth elements enriched relative to the light and heavy ones. That shape is not an original seawater signal. It records diagenesis: during early burial, clay minerals in the host sediment released middle REE into the pore water, and growing apatite took them up. Later burial and recrystallisation then depleted the light REE. Low Y/Ho ratios (28–37) point the same way — a strong terrigenous influence and only a minor seawater (hydrogenous) component.

Using a standard criticality assessment, these concentrations place the Dzhurzhivka conglomerates in the promising to highly promising range as a potential secondary source of critical rare earth elements.

Where did the phosphorus come from?

Continental weathering certainly delivered nutrients to late Ediacaran oceans, but local weathering does not fit the geological evidence as the main phosphorus source here. Instead, the apatite carries positive europium anomalies (Eu/Eu* = 1.35–1.61). Signals like this are typical of high-temperature hydrothermal fluids that altered volcanic rocks on the seafloor — plausibly at mid-ocean ridges or volcanic arcs to the west of SW Baltica.

Our preferred model is that phosphorus-rich deep water welled up onto the margin. That stimulated biological phosphorus cycling, and apatite precipitated in the sediment, possibly coupled to the breakdown of organic matter.

Why it matters

The Dzhurzhivka Beds share key traits with the overlying Kalyus Beds phosphorites — strong middle-REE enrichment and a terrigenous REE source — but sit lower in the succession. They look like a precursor to the more extensive phosphogenesis recorded in the Kalyus Beds. Together they suggest that episodic upwelling of phosphorus-rich deep water, linked to ocean oxygenation, triggered several phosphogenic episodes in southwestern Baltica during the Volyn and Redkino regional stages.

Compared with major Ediacaran phosphorite deposits in China and Brazil, the Dzhurzhivka clasts stand out for their exceptional REE enrichment and positive Eu anomalies. That makes them a distinct end-member of the Ediacaran phosphogenic event — and a reminder that phosphogenesis was patchy in space and time, shaped by local basin dynamics and tectonics rather than a single, globally uniform event.

KEY TERMS
PhosphoriteA sedimentary rock rich in phosphate minerals, mainly apatite.
Rare earth elements (REE)The 15 lanthanides plus yttrium; used in magnets, electronics and catalysts, and as tracers of geological processes.
DiagenesisPhysical and chemical changes in a sediment after deposition and during burial.
Eu anomalyEuropium plotting above or below its neighbours in an REE pattern; a positive anomaly often signals hot hydrothermal fluids.
UpwellingRise of deep, nutrient-rich water onto a shallower shelf.
